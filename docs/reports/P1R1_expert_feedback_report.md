This completed report summarizes how the expert panel evaluated the Phase 1 list of theorists' core tasks and responsibilities after inclusion of all 13 completed responses. ## Consensus rule and updating

During each round, an item is designated as **consensus reached** when its mean rating is >= 4.5 and no more than 25% of valid respondents rate it 3 or lower. The item is then dropped from the next consensus round. Thus, once consensus has been reached on an item, it will not be reevaluated in subsequent rounds.

Written recommendations are used to revise, merge, split, add, or remove items. These decisions are made by the project lead. If you disagree with a decision, please let us know in response to this report.

* 8 items reached consensus and will be dropped from the next consensus round.
* 0 items failed to reach consensus under the predefined rule.

Because all eight items reached consensus, the eight-item structure has been retained. The written comments were used to make minimal wording changes, reduce ambiguity, and clarify the distinction between core theoretical responsibility and delegable execution. 

Some new items were suggested, but others said that the list of core responsibilities was already quite long. Thus, suggested additional items were incorporated into existing items where possible. Suggestions that concerned technical, empirical, infrastructural, applied, or communicative execution were placed in the section **Important but delegable tasks** below. Your comments were also used to update the introduction of the list of core responsibilities of theorists. This clarification adds that the eight items describe core theoretical responsibilities, not tasks that every theorist must personally execute alone. Many tasks require collaboration with empirical researchers, measurement experts, statisticians, computational modelers, domain specialists, designers, or research software engineers. The theorist's non-delegable responsibility is to preserve the theoretical meaning of the work: what is being explained, represented, measured, tested, compared, revised, or applied.

# Item-level decisions

## Item 1: Identification and formation of explananda

**Original item:**  
The theorist identifies, categorizes, and orders the phenomena or capacities to be explained by theory. The theorist distinguishes robust phenomena that warrant explanation from raw data patterns and fleeting effects (e.g., potential false positives) that require further empirical research.

**Survey result:**

* Valid respondents: 13
* Mean rating: 5.62
* Percentage rating 3 or lower: 0.0%
* Consensus status: Consensus reached
* Recommendation categories: 1 revise wording/description; 1 split into multiple items

**Comments:**  
* I would recommend revising the item, because early theory development may also legitimately address emerging, puzzling, or weakly understood patterns, as long as their epistemic status is made explicit. A phenomenon does not need to be fully robust before it can guide theorizing, but the theory should distinguish between well-established explananda, provisional empirical patterns, and speculative targets. Theories do not have to be the endpoint of a research process, but could be an invitation/startingpoint for revision and further development.
* I fully agree with the first sentence. It concerns the terminology and conceptual framework of a theory, or more specifically, how a phenomenon is defined and used within a theory. The second sentence, however, concerns a different issue: the distinction between the terminology or conceptual framework of a theory and data, understood as something external to the theory of interest.
This distinction is important, but these are two separate matters. In my view, the second sentence is more difficult to address because it includes terms such as "robust" and "raw" data patterns. How can one know whether a phenomenon is robust? To judge the robustness of a phenomenon, researchers must have observed relevant patterns in the data repeatedly over time; that is, there must be a collection of facts that is consistent with what the theory claims. Moreover, researchers must agree that this collection of facts counts as evidence for the robustness (instability) of the phenomenon.
For this reason, the second sentence is difficult to apply in practice. It is not clear how researchers can reliably distinguish between a "robust phenomenon" and "raw data patterns."

**Additional comments addressed in this item:**

* **Suggested new items, Comment 2:** There is a tradition of theorizing as a problem solving exercise (advanced by Larry Laudan). The theorist should specify what problem the theory is solving.

**Revised item:**  
The theorist identifies, categorizes, and orders the phenomena, capacities, or problems to be explained by theory. The theorist distinguishes established explananda from provisional patterns, speculative targets, and raw data patterns, while making their epistemic status explicit.

**Explanation:**  
Consensus reached. The item was minimally revised to allow established as well as provisional or emerging explananda, provided their epistemic status is explicit, and to include the idea that theorizing can be oriented toward solving a problem.

## Item 3: Coordinating the relation between theory and measurement

**Original item:**  
The theorist specifies how constructs could, in principle, be detected, measured, or behaviorally evidenced; what counts as valid indicators; and what assumptions connect measures to theoretical entities.

**Survey result:**

* Valid respondents: 13
* Mean rating: 5.54
* Percentage rating 3 or lower: 7.7%
* Consensus status: Consensus reached
* Recommendation categories: 1 revise wording/description

**Comments:**  
* Some constructs are not "detected" or "measured" in the ordinary meaning of these words; for instance, constructs that act as inductive summaries (how many Roman Emperors do you know?) formative measures (SES) or indices (Dow Jones). These constructs are legitimate but do not act as "detectable" attributes because the causal direction does not obey attribute->measure.

**Additional comments addressed in this item:**

* **Important but delegable tasks, Comment 1, part #2:** #2 the theorist should define the measurement problem - measurement experts could solve parts of the technical implementation (i.e., scale construction, item response modeling, reliability estimation, measurement invariance testing, and validation)  
* **Important but delegable tasks, Comment 6:** I rated 3. Coordinating the relation between theory and measurement lower than the other tasks. I think it requires strong empirical experience to derive the most valid measurement techniques. People specializing in theoretical psychology may not necessarily have such experience. When point 2. before is done well, I suspect that measurement is already constrained to such an extent, that specific measurement proposal don't have to be made. Moreover, what if our empirical techniques improve over time. Then, this former proposal is outdated. Hence, I agree that point 3. is important, yet it could be delegated to experimentalists.  
* **Important but delegable tasks, Comment 7:** Yes building tests that manifest good construct validity  
  Meaning that the test empirically behaves in ways that align with key theoretical propositions postulated by the theory  
* **General comments, Comment 5:** Be sure to include that theories can be developed for and evaluated in diverse settings including naturalistic experimental and clinical  
  And using a variety of study designs  
  To evaluate theories of constructs typically construct valid measures are required  
  Evaluation of cons

*Source-data note: The raw Qualtrics CSV also ends this comment at "Evaluation of cons"; the missing ending could not be recovered from the current source file.*

**Revised item:**  
The theorist specifies how constructs could, in principle, be operationalized, indexed, measured, or otherwise evidenced; what counts as valid indicators or indices; and what assumptions connect these to theoretical entities. Technical measurement development can be collaborative or delegated.

**Explanation:**  
Consensus reached. The item was minimally revised to avoid implying that all constructs are detectable attributes; it now covers reflective indicators, formative measures, indices, and other operational representations, while retaining the distinction between theoretical responsibility and delegable technical measurement development.

## Item 4: Proposing, specifying, and connecting mechanisms

**Original item:**  
The theorist proposes and refines mechanisms, distinguishing sketches from more complete schemata and relating psychological explanations to neural, bodily, social, developmental, or environmental levels without reducing one level to another.

**Survey result:**

* Valid respondents: 13
* Mean rating: 5.15
* Percentage rating 3 or lower: 15.4%
* Consensus status: Consensus reached
* Recommendation categories: 4 revise wording/description

**Comments:**  
* "Mechanism" is not a single concept by far and even if it's accepted that it means all the things it could, I think it still may be an optional part. Somebody may want to be functionalist in a way that excludes current mechanistic analysis. Perhaps we can talk more at some point over voice (because it's a can of worms) or I can send you some work I have under development on this -- I think it may help. However, I am also open to interpreting the above as if not prefaced with a "should" or "must".
* In all definitions, so far, I find there to be quite a lot of jargon. That, at least to me, makes the item difficult to read (had to read a couple of times). One reason may be that the sentences are quite long (also in this case). For this particular item: what is considered a mechanism/ when is something a mechanism? I feel this word is been used *a lot* and the more I think about it the less certain I am what we refer to/ whether there is consensus in to what we consider as mechanisms.
* While I think reductionism rarely works, it does work sometimes! The reduction of temperature to average kinetic energy is a pretty cool feat. So I feel it's weird to rule out reduction a priori.
* First, there are several words/concepts that require an explanation: what is meant by "sketch"? What is a "schema" and when is a schema "complete"?
Second, I was not fully sure whether I understood the term "level" here: I think I understand the difference between intraindividual, interindividual, interpersonal, intergroup level (this is the taxonomy of levels I know from social psychology). But I don't really see how "developmental" or "environmental" fits into that picture. Also, I was missing the intraindividual-cognitive/motivational/emotional level in the list.
Third, I wondered whether "reducing one level to another" is necessarily a bad thing -- sometimes, such "inter-level redundancies" can be informative. For instance, an "objective" injustice (on the social/interpersonal level) manifests in anger/outrage (on the intrapersonal level), which results in aggressive behavior (intrapersonal), which affects others (interpersonal). The term "reduce" may be misunderstood in terms of "choose one level for each possible mechanism", which may not always work...

**Additional comments addressed in this item:**

* **Important but delegable tasks, Comment 8, part #1:** \* Many of the things in the list can (in my view) only be achieved in collaboration with other researchers -- for instance, judging what counts as a valid indicator for a construct (you may need a test developer, diagnostician, methodologist here); relating potential mechanisms to each other (you may need a psychologist for the intraindividual level, a sociologist for the macrosocial level, an anthropologist for the evolutionary level, etc.). So many of the things here cannot be done by the theorist alone.

**Revised item:**  
The theorist proposes and refines mechanistic, functional, or process-based explanations, distinguishing preliminary sketches from more specified accounts and relating psychological explanations across cognitive, affective, motivational, behavioral, bodily, neural, social, developmental, and environmental levels.

**Explanation:**  
Consensus reached. The item was revised to reduce jargon, broaden mechanism language to include functional and process-based explanation, clarify the relevant levels of explanation, and avoid ruling out reduction or informative inter-level relations a priori.

The theorist derives implications from theories and models, including explanations of established phenomena, observational predictions, intervention predictions, counterfactual expectations, and expected failures. * Consensus status: Consensus reached
* Recommendation categories: 2 revise wording/description; 4 split into multiple items; 1 other

* Add "qualitative predictions" -- see for why and what I mean:  Guest, O. Martin, A. E., & van Rooij, I. (2026). On Models, Prediction, and Scientific Roles Thereof: Commentary on Sanbonmatsu et al. (2025). Zenodo. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19983695
* I'm not sure I agree with the first 'role', as I think that implications and explanations are quite domain-dependent and that there will be a lot of different ways such a thing could go. I think, therefore, that that role is not (only) the job of the theorist. 

The second part about identifying assumptions and boundary conditions is a somewhat separate role, and while it can be related to the first I think it makes more sense to separate it. Not least, from my perspective, because I have mixed feelings about the first to begin with, but do agree that the second is a role of the theorist. So to have them together makes my response to them quite neutral on balance which masks the nuance that's there.
* Not sure this sounds like an activity in Theorizing, more in using theories
* A lot of things are covered here! Why are these grouped under one item? If there's a reason, can't the item have a more overarching title?
* I do think that everything included in this list here makes perfect sense, but my hunch is that this is a quite broad category, broader than the others, so it may make sense to split it into two or even three sub-categories: (1) identify auxiliary assumptions and boundary conditions (to make the theory / its predictions actually testable), (2) identify cases in which the theory / its predictions have a high chance to be falsified or qualified, (3) keep track of the state of evidence against or in favor of a theory / its predictions.
* Too vague and rambling 
Lead off with theory facilitates comparisons contrasts and evaluation of core propositions in ways that align with its functions. For example descriptive power is measurable via the size and extent of the glossary 
Explanatory power is measurable via the number of phenomena the theory can account for including regularities irregularities correlation prediction causation moderation and mediation 
Predictive power is measurable via classification accuracy variance explained and prediction of rare events/irregularities 
Control is measurable via development of effective efficient interventions including effect size sessions required to effect sufficient change number of reliable improvers vs number of treatment nonresponders and reliable deteriorators creative/ novel insights for treating refractory cases and developing coherent complex interventions that integrate many elements to treat complex cases eg multisystemic treatment refractory intergenerational cases

* **Suggested new items, Comment 6:** See earlier points regarding the functions of theory  
  Theories can be evaluated according to the same criteria for which they were originally developed eg description explanation prediction control fruitfulness  
* **General comments, Comment 1:** '- some items are extremely dense
